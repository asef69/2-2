1747–1759: A Fight Over a Violin String
• 1747: d’Alembert writes down the wave equation for a
plucked string. 1748: Euler finds its normal modes — the
pure shapes that vibrate without changing shape.
• 1753, Daniel Bernoulli makes the leap: every possible
motion of the string is a sum of those modes. He argues
it from physics, and offers no proof.
• 1759, Lagrange demolishes him. A sum of smooth sines
cannot reproduce an arbitrary shape — a string plucked
to a sharp corner, say. Euler agrees. Bernoulli is buried.
• He was right. It took another seventy years to show it.

Laplace, the Tides, and a Machine Made of Brass
• 1775–76: Laplace replaces the old “the Moon pulls a
bulge of water” picture with his tidal equations — the
ocean as a driven, rotating dynamical system.
• The engineering consequence: a tide is a sum of
sinusoids whose frequencies come from astronomy
and whose amplitudes and phases come from the
harbour.
• 1872: William Thomson (Lord Kelvin) builds a
machine of pulleys and wire that adds ten of those
sinusoids and draws the tide — a year of predictions
in about four hours of cranking.
• October 1943: a handwritten letter marked most
urgent reaches Arthur Doodson in Liverpool, asking
for 1944 tides at a place called only “Position Z”. He
gets harmonic constants and no map, and runs them
on Kelvin’s 1872 machine.
• Position Z was Normandy. Those numbers picked the
hour of D-Day.

21 December 1807: The Worst Committee You Could Draw
• Fourier reads On the Propagation of Heat in Solid Bodies to the
Institut de France. The referees are Lagrange, Laplace, Monge
and Lacroix.
• Lagrange is the greatest living analyst — and the same man who
demolished Bernoulli’s sum-of-modes claim 48 years earlier. He
objects again. Publication is blocked.
• The Institut turns the question into the 1811 prize essay. Fourier
enters and wins in January 1812 — with a public censure
attached: “the manner in which the author arrives at these
equations is not exempt of difficulties”. Still no printing.
• So he waits. In 1822 he publishes it himself as Théorie
analytique de la chaleur. He is by then Secretary of the
Académie, and nobody can block him any more.

Lagrange was not being stupid. He was being careful, and on the narrow mathematical point he was
even partly right. He was still wrong about the thing that mattered.

===== page 15 =====
Fourier’s Other Discovery
• Having built a theory of heat, Fourier pointed it at the
largest solid body within reach: the Earth.
• In papers of 1824 and 1827 he worked out how warm a
planet of Earth’s size, at Earth’s distance from the Sun,
ought to be — and found it should be far colder than it is.
• His explanation: the atmosphere lets sunlight in more
easily than it lets heat back out. That is the greenhouse
effect, described in 1824. He compared it to the glass of a
hothouse — which is where the name came from — and
every climate model today is a descendant of it.
One person, two fields
The man who gave signal processing its central tool also
founded climate science. Both fell out of the same stub-
born question about heat.
“Heat, like gravity, penetrates every substance of the
universe.”

So Whose Transform Is It?
• The integral pair was in the air, and at least four people had a
claim.
• Laplace, 1809 gives an integral formula in the Journal de l’École
Polytechnique. Fourier’s own 1822 book credits him for it: “Elle a
été donnée par M. Laplace …nous ne faisons que l’appliquer” —
it was given by Mr Laplace; we merely apply it.
• Fourier, 1811–1822 writes the forward and inverse sine and
cosine transforms explicitly, and finds that differentiating n
times multiplies the transform by (jω)n.
• Cauchy, 1815 and Poisson, 1816 publish the same formula
independently — and, unlike Fourier, prove that the integral
converges.

===== page 20 =====
The Argument Nobody Could Settle — and What It Built
• Lagrange’s objection would not die, because “does this series
actually converge?” turned out to be genuinely hard. Answering
it rebuilt mathematics.
• 1829, Dirichlet gives the first honest convergence conditions,
and shows that at a jump the series converges to the midpoint.
• 1854, Riemann needs a better notion of “area under a curve” for
trigonometric series — and invents the Riemann integral.
• 1870, Cantor asks a small question: can a function have two
different trigonometric series? Chasing the exceptional sets
where his proof failed, he invents set theory.
• 1902, Lebesgue builds a new integral, again with Fourier series
in mind. 1966, Carleson finally settles the question — 207 years
after Lagrange raised it.

The Overshoot That Would Not Go Away
• 1848: an English mathematician, Henry Wilbraham,
notices the spike at a jump, analyses it correctly,
publishes — and is comprehensively ignored for the next
66 years.
• 1898: Albert Michelson — of Michelson–Morley — builds

goes away — it only gets narrower.

===== page 25 =====
1805: Gauss Gets There First, and Tells Nobody
• Gauss is chasing the newly discovered asteroids Pallas and
Juno. He has a handful of noisy sightings and needs to
interpolate a periodic orbit through them.
• Doing the trigonometric coefficients by hand is brutal, so he
finds a shortcut: split the samples into two interleaved halves,
transform each, then combine. Then do it again. And again.
• That is the radix-2 decimation-in-time FFT — the identical
algorithm you will derive later in this course.
• He writes it in a private notebook, in Latin, in a notation of his
own. He never publishes it. It surfaces only in 1866, in his
posthumous collected works, where essentially nobody reads
it.
Two years before Fourier
The fast Fourier transform was written down before the
Fourier transform was — and then lost for 160 years.
Carl Friedrich Gauss

Why Anyone Cared in 1963
• After 1945 the United States offered to give up its arsenal
if others would forgo theirs. The offer failed, and the arms
race started.
• By the early 1960s both sides wanted a test ban, and both
had the same obstacle: verification.

===== page 30 =====
The Part That Stings
• The algorithm worked. The monitoring network it made possible got built. But by 1965 the moment had
gone: the United Kingdom, France and China had all joined the club, and the political will for a
comprehensive ban went with them.
• The Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty was not opened for signature until 1996 — and it has still
not entered into force.
• It is watched anyway. The CTBTO’s International Monitoring System is 321 stations and 16 laboratories
across 89 countries, 170 of them seismic, running Fourier transforms day and night — exactly the machine
Garwin wanted, thirty years late.
The counterfactual
Veritasium’s claim — and it is a claim, not a proof — is that an FFT a decade earlier might have made a
comprehensive test ban verifiable while it was still politically possible. Either way, one thing is certain:
the algorithm arrived too late for the treaty, and exactly in time for everything else.

===== page 33 =====
Oliver Heaviside (1850–1925): The Outsider
• Scarlet fever left him partly deaf as a child. He left school at
sixteen, taught himself Morse, electricity, Danish and German,
and worked as a telegraph clerk until the deafness ended that
too. Then he simply stopped having a job, and did physics
alone in a back room for decades.
• He reduced Maxwell’s twenty equations in twenty variables to
the four every physics student now learns, and coined
impedance, inductance and reactance.
• And he invented operational calculus: write p for d/dt, treat it
as ordinary algebra, and solve circuit equations as if they were
fractions. It worked; telegraph engineers used it daily. It was
also, by 1890s standards, entirely unjustified — and the Royal
Society rejected the paper for “irredeemable inadequacies in
proof”.
• He was right about the sky too: in 1902 he predicted a
conducting layer in the upper atmosphere that reflects radio
waves. Confirmed in 1923, it is still called the Heaviside layer.

1947: Radar, Sampled Data, and a Topologist
• Wartime tracking radar does not see continuously. It sees in
pulses: one sample per sweep of the antenna.
• A control loop closed around such a sensor is not governed by
a differential equation but by a difference equation — and the
Laplace transform alone does not handle it cleanly.
• At the MIT Radiation Laboratory, Witold Hurewicz solves it in
the 1947 volume Theory of Servomechanisms by reviving the
old generating-function idea for sampled sequences.
• Hurewicz was a topologist by trade — the Hurewicz theorem and
the Hurewicz fibration are his — who had wandered into control
engineering because of the war. He died in 1956 after falling
from a Mayan pyramid at Uxmal, on a conference excursion.
