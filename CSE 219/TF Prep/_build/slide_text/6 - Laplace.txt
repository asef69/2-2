===== page 4 =====
Introduction to the Laplace Transform
• Why general complex exponentials? We learnt that a broad class of functions can be represented as a
sum of complex exponentials ejωt. Also, complex exponentials are eigenfunctions of LTI systems, i.e.
y(t) = H(jω)ejωt for x(t) = ejωt.
• Using the properties above, we expressed both input and output of an LTI system as a sum of complex
exponentials.
• However, the eigenfunction property holds for any arbitrary complex value s = σ + jω.
• This broader property leads to a generalization of the continuous-time Fourier transform known as the
Laplace Transform.
• Key Advantages of the Laplace Transform:
• It can analyze broad classes of signals and unstable systems where the Fourier transform does not exist.
• It provides deeper insights into the stability or instability of systems.
• It maintains powerful algebraic properties, forming an essential set of tools for system analysis, especially
feedback systems.

The s-plane and Region of Convergence (ROC)
• The Laplace transform depends on the complex
variable s = σ + jω.
• We visualize this using a 2D plot called the s-plane:
• Horizontal axis (σ): Real part of s.
• Vertical axis (jω): Imaginary part of s.
• Region of Convergence (ROC): The specific set of values
in the s-plane for which the integral X(s) converges to
a finite value.
• Unlike Fourier Transform, the ROC is an important part of
the Laplace Transform.
• As we saw in the previous two example, even though the
algebraic expression for X(s) was the same, the ROC was
different.

Rational Laplace Transforms
• Many important LTI systems and signals have Laplace transforms that are rational functions of s, meaning
they are ratios of two polynomials in s.
• For a signal x(t) with a rational Laplace transform, we can express X(s) as:
X(s) = N(s)
D(s)
• Zeros are the values of s for which N(s) = 0 (so X(s) = 0). We denote roots of the numerator with an o
(◦) on the s-plane.
• Poles are the values of s for which D(s) = 0 (so X(s) →∞). We denote roots of the denominator with
an x (×).

Justification: The Laplace transform converges if x(t)e−st is absolutely integrable. If σ + jω is in

Property 2: For rational Laplace transforms, the ROC does not contain any poles.
Justification: By definition, X(s) evaluates to infinity at a pole. Since convergence requires X(s) to
be finite, the integral naturally diverges at the exact location of any pole.

===== page 24 =====
ROC Properties 7 and 8: Rational Transforms
Property 7: If the Laplace transform X(s) is rational, then its ROC is bounded by poles or
extends to infinity. In addition, no poles are contained in the ROC.
Justification: Rational transforms are combinations of exponentials (we will see this soon). And we
saw already that the ROC of an exponential is bounded by a pole (e.g. e−atu(t) has a pole at −a
and the ROC extends from −a to ∞).
As the ROC of a linear combination of signals is the intersection of the ROCs of the individual
signals, the ROC of a rational transform is bounded by poles or extends to infinity.
Property 8: If X(s) is rational, then:
• If x(t) is right-sided, the ROC is the region to the right of the rightmost pole.
• If x(t) is left-sided, the ROC is the region to the left of the leftmost pole.
Justification: Combination of property 4 and 5 with property 7.
